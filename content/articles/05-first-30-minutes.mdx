<Section id="setup" title="Setup (5 minutes)" />

Before you touch a fader, make sure you can hear both decks in your headphones. This sounds obvious, but it's the step most beginners skip — and it's why their first mixes sound like a car crash.

**What you need to hear:**
- **Deck 1** (the track playing now) in your headphones and on the main output
- **Deck 2** (the next track) only in your headphones, not on the main output

If the audience can hear deck 2 before you bring it in, you've ruined the surprise. Set your mixer so the cue button on deck 2 sends that track to your headphones only, while deck 1 plays to the room.

<Note title="Gain staging">Set each channel's trim so the level meter peaks in the yellow, not the red. Red means clipping — it sounds harsh and can damage speakers over time. Green is too quiet; you'll introduce hiss when you turn it up.</Note>

**Headphone split:** Most DJ controllers and mixers have a headphone mix knob. Turn it all the way to "cue" so you hear only deck 2 in your headphones. Once you're ready to bring deck 2 in, sweep it toward "master" so you hear both decks together.

<Section id="first-transition" title="Your first transition (15 minutes)" />

Load the same track on both decks. Yes, the same track — this removes variables so you can focus on the mechanics, not track selection.

1. Press play on deck 1. Let it play to the room.
2. Press cue on deck 2 so you hear it in your headphones only.
3. Use the pitch fader on deck 2 to match deck 1's tempo. Watch the BPM display and nudge until both read the same number.
4. When both tracks are at the same BPM, use the jog wheel to align the beats. Listen for the "galloping" sound — that's the kicks hitting at different times. Nudge the jog wheel until the kicks line up perfectly.
5. Slowly bring up deck 2's channel fader while bringing down deck 1's. Do this over 4-8 beats, not all at once.
6. When deck 1's fader is all the way down, you've completed your first transition.

Congratulations — you just did a DJ transition. It probably sounded rough. That's normal. Do it 20 more times with the same track. Then try it with two different tracks.

<Section id="eq-basics" title="EQ during your first transitions (10 minutes)" />

Once you can do a basic transition, start using the EQ to make it smoother. The EQ knobs (low/mid/high) cut or boost frequency ranges on each channel.

**A simple EQ transition:**
- As deck 1 plays, cut its bass (low EQ) all the way down
- Bring in deck 2 with its bass also cut
- Once deck 2 is playing, bring its bass back up
- Cut deck 1 entirely

This prevents two basslines from fighting each other, which is what makes beginner mixes sound muddy.

<Section id="cueing-and-headphones" title="Cueing technique (5 minutes)" />

Cueing is how you listen to the next track before the audience hears it. Two rules:

1. **Cue one deck at a time.** Don't cue both decks simultaneously — you won't be able to hear either one clearly.
2. **Use one ear on, one ear off.** Keep one headphone cup on your ear and one off so you can hear the main output in the room while previewing the next track in your headphones. This is how you know when the beats are aligned.

<Section id="next-steps" title="Next steps (10 minutes)" />

- Practice the same transition with two different tracks (not the same track)
- Try using the crossfader instead of the channel faders
- Experiment with the EQ — cut the bass on the incoming track, then swap
- Set a hot cue at the start of each track's drop so you can jump straight to it
- Record yourself on your phone and listen back — you'll hear mistakes you didn't notice while mixing
