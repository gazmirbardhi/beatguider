<Section id="why-eq" title="Why EQ matters more than fancy transitions" />

A clean EQ swap sounds better than the most elaborate effect-chain transition done badly. When two tracks are playing simultaneously, their bass frequencies collide — the result is muddy, boomy sound that makes your mix feel amateur. Kill the bass on one track and the mix instantly sounds professional.

**The problem:** Most tracks are mastered with prominent bass. When two basslines play at once, they fight for the same frequency space (typically 60-150 Hz). Your speakers can't reproduce both cleanly, so they cancel each other out or distort.

**The solution:** EQ lets you carve out space for each track. Instead of fighting frequencies, you hand off the bass from one track to the other cleanly.

<Note title="The golden rule">Only one track's bass should be audible at any time. Cut the bass on the outgoing track as you bring in the incoming track's bass.</Note>

<Section id="eq-controls" title="Understanding your EQ knobs" />

Most DJ mixers and controllers have three EQ knobs per channel:

- **Low (bass):** Controls roughly 20 Hz to 150 Hz — kick drums, basslines, sub-bass. This is where most of the energy lives.
- **Mid:** Controls roughly 150 Hz to 1.5 kHz — vocals, snare hits, guitar body, synth warmth.
- **High (treble):** Controls roughly 1.5 kHz to 20 kHz — hi-hats, cymbals, sibilance, air.

Each knob is a **kill switch** when turned fully counterclockwise — it removes that frequency band entirely. At center (12 o'clock), the frequency passes through unaffected.

<Section id="the-technique" title="The classic EQ swap" />

<Step n={1} title="Start with both bass EQs at center">

Both channel faders up, both bass EQ knobs at 12 o'clock. You should hear the mud — two basslines fighting for the same space.

</Step>

<Step n={2} title="Cut the outgoing bass">

Slowly turn the bass EQ on the outgoing track counterclockwise until it's fully cut. Notice how the mix instantly tightens up — the low end stops fighting and the incoming track's bass has room to breathe.

</Step>

<Step n={3} title="Balance the mids and highs">

Gently adjust the mid and high EQs on both tracks to balance the blend. Cut the mids on the incoming track slightly if it sounds too vocal-heavy. Roll off the highs on the outgoing track if it sounds too bright against the new track. You're sculpting the blend, not just switching.

</Step>

<Step n={4} title="Bring in the incoming bass">

When you're ready to commit to the new track, bring its bass EQ back to center while simultaneously pulling the channel fader down on the outgoing track. The bass handoff should be smooth — if you hear a jump or dip, adjust the timing.

</Step>

<Section id="when-to-use-eq" title="When to use EQ during a mix" />

**On every transition.** The EQ swap isn't a technique you use occasionally — it's the foundation of every professional mix. Even a simple fade between two tracks sounds better with EQ involved.

**Specific situations:**
- **Dropping a new track on the 1:** Cut the outgoing bass on beat 1, bring in the incoming bass on beat 1 of the next phrase.
- **Building energy:** Gradually boost the incoming track's highs over 8 bars to create anticipation before the drop.
- **Fading out:** Cut the bass first, then the mids, then the highs — the track disappears cleanly instead of abruptly.

<Section id="common-mistakes" title="Common beginner mistakes" />

- **Cutting both basses at once:** You end up with no bass and a hollow-sounding mix. Always leave one bass audible.
- **Turning EQs all the way up:** Boosting frequencies adds noise and can cause distortion. It's usually better to cut than to boost.
- **Ignoring the mids:** Beginners focus only on bass, but clashing vocals and snares in the midrange can ruin a mix just as badly.

<Section id="practice" title="How to practice" />

Load two tracks you know well — similar BPM, similar genre. Practice the EQ swap 50 times. Not 5 — 50. The muscle memory needs to be automatic so you can focus on reading the crowd, not your hands.

**A good practice routine:**
1. Pick two tracks with similar energy levels
2. Do a full EQ swap transition
3. Record it on your phone
4. Listen back — did the bass handoff sound smooth, or was there a jump?
5. Repeat with different track combinations until it feels effortless
