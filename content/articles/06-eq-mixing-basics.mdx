<Section id="why-eq" title="Why EQ matters more than fancy transitions" />

A clean EQ swap sounds better than the most elaborate effect-chain transition done badly. When two tracks are playing simultaneously, their bass frequencies collide — the result is mud. Kill the bass on one track and the mix instantly sounds professional.

<Note title="The golden rule">Only one track's bass should be audible at any time. Cut the bass on the outgoing track as you bring in the incoming track's bass.</Note>

<Section id="the-technique" title="The technique" />

<Step n={1} title="Start with both bass EQs at center">

Both channel faders up, both bass EQ knobs at 12 o'clock. You should hear the mud — two basslines fighting.

</Step>

<Step n={2} title="Cut the outgoing bass">

Slowly turn the bass EQ on the outgoing track counterclockwise until it's fully cut. Notice how the mix instantly tightens up.

</Step>

<Step n={3} title="Swap the mids and highs">

Gently adjust the mid and high EQs to balance the two tracks. You're sculpting the blend, not just switching.

</Step>

<Step n={4} title="Bring in the incoming bass">

When you're ready to commit to the new track, bring its bass EQ back to center while simultaneously pulling the channel fader down on the outgoing track.

</Step>

<Section id="practice" title="How to practice" />

Load two tracks you know well. Practice the EQ swap 50 times. Not 5 — 50. The muscle memory needs to be automatic so you can focus on reading the crowd, not your hands.
