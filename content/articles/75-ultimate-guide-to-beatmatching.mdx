Beatmatching is the foundation of DJing. It is the skill that separates DJs who play music from DJs who *perform* music. Whether you use a beginner controller, pro CDJs, or classic vinyl turntables, understanding how to lock two tracks together in perfect time is non-negotiable.

In this ultimate guide, you'll learn beatmatching across every format:

## What Beatmatching Actually Is and Why It Matters

**Beatmatching is the process of synchronizing two tracks so their beats play at exactly the same time.** It has two components:

1. **Tempo matching** — getting both tracks to the same BPM using the tempo/pitch fader
2. **Phase alignment** — getting the downbeats of both tracks to hit simultaneously using cue buttons and jog wheels

<Note title="Beatmatching vs. Mixing — What's the Difference?">

Beatmatching is the technical skill of syncing two tracks. Mixing is the art of blending them together creatively — using EQ, faders, and effects. You need beatmatching before you can mix. If your beats aren't locked, your mix will sound sloppy no matter how good your EQ skills are.

</Note>

When you beatmatch correctly, the audience hears a seamless flow of music. When you get it wrong, you get what DJs call a **train wreck** — both tracks audibly out of sync, clashing beats, and confused dancers.

---

## How Beatmatching Works: The Core Principle

**No matter what equipment you use, the core principle is always the same: match tempo, then align phase.**

When you load a track into a DJ deck, it has a BPM (beats per minute). Your first track is playing at its BPM. Your second track has a different BPM. The tempo fader lets you speed up or slow down the second track until both are running at the same speed.

But matching BPM alone isn't enough. Even if both tracks are at 128 BPM, they might be out of phase — one track's downbeat hitting a fraction of a second before or after the other. This is where **cueing** and **jog wheel nudging** come in.

| Step | Action | Tool Used | What You're Achieving |
|------|--------|-----------|----------------------|
| 1 | Match the BPM of the incoming track to the playing track | Tempo fader / Pitch control | Both tracks run at the same speed |
| 2 | Align the downbeat of the incoming track | Cue button | Both tracks start in phase |
| 3 | Fine-tune alignment | Jog wheel (controllers/CDJs) or platter/spindle (vinyl) | Perfect sync maintained |

## Beatmatching on DJ Controllers and CDJs

**This is the most common way DJs learn today. Modern controllers and CDJs give you BPM readouts, waveforms, and optional sync — but the fundamentals are the same as ever.**

### Why You Always Adjust the Second Track, Not the First

When your current song is playing and people are dancing, adjusting its BPM can affect the vibe. Affecting a track that hasn't been introduced yet is always the better move.

Adjust the **tempo fader** on the deck with the incoming track. Never touch the playing track's tempo once the crowd is dancing.

### Step One: Getting Into Rhythm With the Cue Button

Just matching BPMs isn't enough — timing is everything. Even pros skip this step sometimes and regret it.

The **cue button** allows you to start a track temporarily. What pros do is tap the cue button right before playing their next track in, to get into rhythm with the current track. The goal is to cue the downbeat of the next track precisely when the downbeat of the current track lands.

Once you're feeling prepared and in sync with the rhythm, go ahead and play the next track. If you still get your timing massively wrong, simply press the cue button and try again.

### Step Two: Fine-Tuning With the Jog Wheel

Perfectly aligned tracks won't happen every time — that's exactly what jog wheels are for. Jog wheels function to temporarily speed up or slow down a song:

| Jog Wheel Direction | Effect |
|--------------------|--------|
| Nudge clockwise | Speeds the track up temporarily |
| Nudge anticlockwise | Slows the track down temporarily |

After cueing and playing your track, the next and final step is to fine-tune your beat match using the jog wheel. When using CDJs and playing with different genres and BPMs, the approach remains exactly the same.

### How Beatmatching Works in a Real Club Scenario

Deck one is playing with its volume all the way up while deck two is playing with its volume down. The audience hears only deck one. But if you press deck two's **headphone cue button**, you hear deck two in your headphones privately.

This means you can spend as long as you need beatmatching deck two in your headphones while the audience hears only what's playing on deck one. When your beatmatch is perfect, you bring up the volume fader smoothly.

<Note title="The Pro Beatmatching Workflow">

1. With the current song playing, load your next track and bring its volume all the way down
2. Using the tempo fader, adjust the next track's BPM to match the current track's BPM
3. Press the headphone cue button so you can hear the next track privately
4. Get into rhythm by repeatedly pressing the next track's cue button
5. Once ready, play your next track in with the volume fader still down
6. Perfect your beat match by jog-wheeling the track
7. Bring the next track's volume up and mix using EQ

</Note>

### Understanding Sync: BPM Sync vs Beat Sync

You need to know what's being synced — otherwise sync will cause more problems than it solves.

- **BPM Sync**: When you press the sync button on a deck, the track's BPM synchronises with the master deck's BPM. You still need to align the phase yourself using the jog wheel.
- **Beat Sync**: The track's BPM follows the master deck AND the track aligns in phase automatically.

| Sync Mode | What It Does | Best Used When |
|-----------|-------------|----------------|
| BPM Sync | Matches tempo only | You want manual phase control |
| Beat Sync | Matches tempo and phase | Quick mixing or beginners |

**Pro tip:** To set a deck as master on most controllers, simply hold the sync button on that deck. All other decks will then follow that master deck's tempo.

### The Two Biggest Mistakes DJs Make With Sync

**Not analysing tracks and not checking beat grids** are the two mistakes that break sync every time.

An unanalysed track will show a misaligned beat grid — the white lines are supposed to align with the drum beat, but sometimes DJ software gets it wrong. If you have tracks with misaligned beat grids, you have to manually fix them.

**How to Fix Beat Grids on Rekordbox:**
With your track loaded, zoom in on the waveform. Drag the downbeat to the red line — you want that red line to be right before the beat starts. Then go to that section and press the Set button. Test that the white lines align with the drum beat to confirm everything is correct.

**How to Fix Beat Grids on Serato:**
With your track loaded, press **Edit Grid**. Then drag the downbeat to the white line. Press **Clear**, then press **Set**, then press **Save**.

## Beatmatching on Vinyl Turntables

**Vinyl beatmatching is the hardest but most rewarding way to learn. No BPM counters, no waveforms, no sync — just your ears and your hands.**

### The Equipment You Need

Mixing vinyl comes down to two turntables, a simple two-channel mixer, and the right setup. If you're just starting out, keep it as simple as possible — a basic mixer with two volume faders lets you focus on learning the turntable and pitch control.

<Caution title="Keep It Simple to Start">

If you're just starting out mixing vinyl, use a simple two-channel mixer with two volume faders. This way you can actually focus on learning how to use the turntable and the pitch control to beat match two tracks together.

</Caution>

### Understanding the Turntable: Slipmats, Platters and Torque

The **slipmat** is one of the most important parts of turntable beatmatching. When you press start on the turntable and it spins, you can hold the slipmat and the platter will continue moving underneath. Even though your hand is on the record and the record is still, the platter underneath is still spinning.

The **Technics SL-1210** has a really high torque — it reaches full speed almost instantly. Cheaper turntables have lower torque, meaning the track takes longer to get up to speed. Both are workable for learning.

<Note title="Low Torque Turntables Are Fine for Learning">

It is totally possible to learn to beat match on turntables with low torque. Back in the 90s, I started on a terrible pair of Numark blue belt-drive turntables, but I learned to mix no problem. It was more difficult, but if you're using cheaper low torque turntables, don't worry — you can still learn all of these skills.

</Note>

### Understanding 33 RPM, 45 RPM and the Pitch Control

On the right-hand side of the deck, you've got the **pitch adjustment**. This is the most used part of the turntable when you're beatmatching, because it allows you to get two tracks running at exactly the same speed.

| Pitch Control Range | Effect on Tempo |
|---|---|
| +8% | Fastest — track plays at 108% of original BPM |
| 0% (centre, green light) | Original speed |
| -8% | Slowest — track plays at 92% of original BPM |

<Note title="Rule of Thumb for Pitch">

Try not to play a track at below zero (slower than its original speed). Hearing a track playing slower than intended just doesn't sound quite right. Playing at a faster speed adds energy. It's a personal preference, but most vinyl DJs stick to this rule.

</Note>

### How to Physically Control the Record While Mixing

| Control Method | How It Works | Difficulty |
|---------------|-------------|------------|
| **Spindle push/pull** | Push or pull the centre spindle to micro-adjust | Beginner-friendly |
| **Platter rub** | Rub finger against the platter edge to slow down | Requires light touch |
| **Push platter forward** | Push the platter edge to speed up | Requires light touch |
| **Touch the record** | Push or tap the vinyl directly | Advanced — risk of skipping |

Use a light touch when physically controlling the record. The amount of pressure varies depending on your turntable's torque. A lighter touch is needed on low-torque turntables.

<Caution title="Touching the Record Is Risky for Beginners">

If you need to speed the record up, push the record forward. To slow it down, tap the record. This works for experienced DJs who've spent thousands of hours with vinyl, but for beginners it can easily cause the record to skip. Use the platter or spindle instead.

</Caution>

### How to Cue Up a Record Using the Baby Scratch Technique

Cueing up correctly from the first kick drum is the foundation of every clean mix on vinyl. Put the needle at the start of the record, press play, and find the **first kick drum**.

Perform a **baby scratch** — this allows you to keep time with the record and let the track go by releasing the record at the right moment. This is the foundation of all vinyl mixing.

### The Beatmatching Process on Vinyl

Adjust the pitch control to line up the beats of this track with the track on the other deck. Listen for the **drift** — is the incoming track running faster or slower than the playing track?

1. Listen in your headphones. Adjust the pitch control to get into the rough tempo range.
2. Fine-tune by rubbing the platter or nudging the spindle.
3. If the tracks drift apart again, you need to adjust the pitch control further.
4. Because these are analog pieces of equipment, they'll always drift slightly — you must stay engaged and keep micro-adjusting.

<Note title="The Beauty of Imperfection">

Perfection is kind of impossible because we're using two analog pieces of equipment, but honestly, that's the beauty of it. Every new record is like a mathematical problem that needs solving.

</Note>

### The Pitch Ride Method: Accelerate Your Learning

**Riding the pitch — without touching the record at all** — is a fairly advanced skill that will accelerate your learning faster than anything else.

Perform the mix, but do not touch the record, the spindle, or the platter at all. Use only the **pitch control** to speed up and slow down the track. This requires real concentration and lots of **micro adjustments**. You'll understand exactly how the pitch control should move to lock two tracks together much faster than if you were manually speeding it up and slowing it down.

<Note title="Be Patient With Yourself">

If your beat matching takes longer than mine to get right, that's totally fine. I've been doing this for a very, very long time. It might take you 1, 2, or 3 minutes to get the track locked into the same tempo, and that is totally normal.

</Note>

### Common Mistakes When Beatmatching on Vinyl

**Mistake 1: Moving the Pitch Control Too Aggressively**
If you feel the tracks are nearly locked, don't move the pitch slider too aggressively — you'll undo all your hard work. Small adjustments only.

**Mistake 2: Touching the Record Too Hard**
You only need a very light touch on the record. Too heavy a hand will stop the platter. A light touch for the baby scratch, then let go.

**Mistake 3: Panic Nudging the Record**
First, find the rough range where the track needs to be. Then start making micro adjustments. Don't constantly speed it up and slow down in a panic.

**Mistake 4: Not Listening Long Enough in Headphones**
It takes time to work out whether the track needs speeding up or slowing down — especially when they're already close. Spend enough time listening.

**Mistake 5: Using Headphones Incorrectly**
The best technique is one headphone on, one off. Listen to the cue track in one ear and the playing track through the other ear with the room sound.

## How to Manually Beatmatch by Ear: 5 Progressive Drills

**These five practice drills progress in difficulty. Start with Drill 1 and work through in order.**

<Note title="Drill Progression">

- Start with Drill 1 and work through in order
- Each drill builds on the skills from the previous one
- Only move to the next drill when you feel comfortable
- The final drill simulates a real club mixing scenario

</Note>

### Drill 1: Match the BPMs and Press Play in Time

Set both tracks to the same BPM (e.g., 128 BPM). Press play on one track, then practice pressing play on the other track in phrase — every **32 beats** signals a new phrase with a cymbal crash.

Once both tracks are running, nudge the jog wheel to align the beats. Nudge clockwise to speed up, anticlockwise to slow down.

<Caution title="Look Away From the Screen">

Try not to look at your screen. It's very easy to look at the waveforms and match the lines up. But if you want to train your ears, look away from the screen and just use your ears.

</Caution>

### Drill 2: Beatmatch With a 2 BPM Difference

Set one track to 128 BPM and the other to 126 BPM. You know you need to speed the left track up by around 2 BPM. Do not look at your DJ software screen. Rely fully on your ears.

Use a combination of nudging the jog wheel and moving the tempo fader in the same direction. If the beats keep going out, move the tempo fader a little more. The closer you get, the slower it takes for the beats to drift.

| Signal | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| Beats drift apart slowly | You're close — make smaller adjustments |
| Beats drift apart quickly | You're far off — make bigger tempo fader moves |
| Beats go further apart after a nudge | You've overshot — change direction |

### Drill 3: Beatmatch With a 5 BPM Difference

With a bigger gap, the beats go out of time much faster. You'll need bigger adjustments on the jog wheel and tempo fader. As you get closer, make smaller adjustments.

Practice in both directions — speeding up and slowing down the incoming track.

### Drill 4: Beatmatch Without Looking at the BPMs

Move both tempo faders to random positions. You don't know if you need to go faster or slower or how big the gap is. Use trial and error — press play and move one track faster. If the beats get closer, keep moving in that direction.

<Caution title="This Takes Time">

This skill takes time. It's not something you do once and then it's completed. It takes a while to train your ears and get used to using the jog wheel to adjust the beats. Over time it will become more natural and automatic.

</Caution>

### Drill 5: Beatmatch Two Full Real Tracks by Ear

This is the hardest drill. Two real tracks with basslines, instruments, and vocals — no drum-only simplicity. Start at the same BPM, move through 2 BPM, 5 BPM, and finally random BPMs where you don't know where they are.

Give yourself a time frame from the start of the track to the end. Try and beatmatch without looking at the screens. If you get comfortable, try linking several tracks together in a playlist without ever looking at the screen.

<Note title="Why Manual Beatmatching Still Matters">

This skill isn't used much anymore in modern DJing, but knowing it builds real confidence. However, when DJing live, feeling confident that if anything goes wrong you can always use your ears to manually beat match is genuinely valuable. It is a repetition game — you have to keep doing it over and over.

</Note>

## Training Your Ears: The Phasing Exercise

Here's a powerful exercise: load the same track on both decks. Beatmatch them perfectly. When both tracks are locked in time, you'll hear a **phasing effect** — the sound becomes wider and more resonant. This phasing is your signal that you've achieved perfect alignment.

If you want to challenge yourself, purposely mis-time playing your next track and then figure out where to jog-wheel it. No matter how bad your timing is, as long as your tracks are in the same BPM, you can always fix things up by jog-wheeling.

## Common Beatmatching Mistakes Across All Formats

| Mistake | Why It Happens | The Fix |
|---------|---------------|---------|
| Train wreck on mix-in | Didn't listen long enough in headphones | Spend 15-30 seconds confirming alignment before raising fader |
| Tracks drift mid-mix | Tempo fader slightly off | Keep one hand near the jog wheel/pitch for the first 30 seconds |
| Wrong track goes out of sync | Adjusted the playing track instead of incoming | Always adjust the track that hasn't reached the crowd yet |
| Can't hear if track is faster or slower | Ears aren't trained yet | The phasing exercise — load same track on both decks |
| Sync button makes things worse | Beat grid is misaligned | Manually fix the grid on Rekordbox or Serato |

<Section id="faq" title="Frequently asked questions" />

### What is beatmatching in DJing?

Beatmatching is the technique of synchronizing the tempo and phase of two tracks so their beats play perfectly together. It involves adjusting the BPM (beats per minute) of the incoming track to match the playing track, then aligning the downbeats so both tracks play in sync. It is the foundational skill of DJing.

#### Do I need to learn to beatmatch by ear in 2026?

While modern DJ software has BPM counters and Beat Sync, learning to beatmatch by ear builds genuine confidence and prepares you for equipment failures. If your sync button fails or a beat grid is misaligned, knowing how to beatmatch manually can save your set. It also deepens your understanding of phrasing and timing.

#### Is beatmatching harder on vinyl than on controllers?

Yes, beatmatching on vinyl is significantly harder because you don't have BPM readouts, beat grids, or sync buttons. You must rely entirely on your ears, the pitch control, and physical manipulation of the record. However, many DJs find vinyl beatmatching more satisfying and musical.

#### What is the difference between BPM sync and Beat Sync?

BPM Sync only matches the tempo of the slave deck to the master — you still need to manually align the phase using the jog wheel. Beat Sync matches both tempo and phase automatically. BPM Sync is preferred by pros who want creative control, while Beat Sync is better for beginners.

#### How long does it take to learn to beatmatch?

Most DJs can learn basic beatmatching on a controller within 2-4 weeks of daily practice. Manual beatmatching by ear typically takes 1-3 months to feel comfortable. Vinyl beatmatching can take 3-6 months or longer to achieve consistent, clean mixes.

#### What are the most common beatmatching mistakes?

The most common mistakes are: not listening in headphones long enough before mixing, moving the tempo fader too aggressively, panic-nudging the jog wheel, not fixing beat grids before using sync, and touching the vinyl record too hard when mixing on turntables.

#### Should I use sync or beatmatch manually?

Use both. Learn manual beatmatching as a foundational skill so you understand what sync is doing under the hood. Then use sync for convenience during long sets and save manual intervention for when tracks need fine-tuning or when sync fails. The best DJs know how to do both.

#### Can I beatmatch on any DJ controller?

Yes, every DJ controller has the basic tools for beatmatching: tempo faders to adjust BPM, jog wheels to nudge tracks in phase, and cue buttons to align downbeats. The technique is the same whether you're using a $200 beginner controller or a $2,000 club setup.
